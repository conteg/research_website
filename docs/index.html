Job market paper

The Distributional Effects of Paid Parental Leave Policies on Fertility and Child Human Capital

How do fertility responses to paid parental leave shape parental investment and children’s human capital?

Read paper
Abstract

This paper studies the implications of paid parental leave (PPL) policies on fertility and children’s human capital. Using micro-level data, I show that the introduction of PPL in New Jersey is associated with an increase in fertility. To investigate the implications of these policy-induced responses for children, I develop a heterogeneous agent model that combines endogenous fertility and multiperiod parental investments in children’s human capital. I structurally estimate the model parameters to match key moments characterizing the U.S. economy. I then use the model to conduct a counterfactual exercise by simulating the introduction of the New Jersey policy at the national level. The model predicts an increase in higher-order births in response to the policy implementation, with larger effects among middle-income families and among low-educated women. The increases in fertility lead parents to dilute per-child investment, negatively affecting children’s human capital through the mechanism of the quantity-quality trade-off. Finally, I show that in the absence of fertility responses, PPL would lead to higher investment in children, with positive implications for their human capital, underscoring the central role of fertility in shaping policy effects.

Parental Leave Policies and Fertility: the Emotional Cost of Maternal Care

With Herdis Steingrimsdottir (CBS)

How does family support mediate the effects of longer parental leave on subsequent births?

Best Paper Award · ISEWE and IEA · IEA Annual Conference 2024

Read paper
Abstract

This paper examines how non-monetary costs of maternal care influence women’s fertility decisions. We leverage a Danish reform that exogenously extended parental leave entitlements—raising average leave uptake by 5.3 weeks—to investigate heterogeneous effects on subsequent births based on the presence and employment status of women’s own mothers and mothers-in-law. Using a instrumental variable (IV) startegy, we find that among women whose mothers were non-working and able to provide childcare support during leave, the extended benefit increases the probability of a second birth within three years by 11 percentage points (rising to 23 points for first-time mothers). In contrast, women lacking family support—those whose mothers and mothers-in-law remained employed, were deceased or living abroad—experience an 11 percentage point decline in the likelihood of another child. We interpret these results as evidence that family support, particularly from the maternal grandmother, mediates the impact of longer leave on fertility by altering the non-monetary (emotional) costs of childcare. Extended leave can exacerbate emotional burdens if it prolongs periods of isolation for women recovering from childbirth as sole caregivers, whereas if complemented with family support, extended leave mitigate these costs and encourages higher fertility. Our findings highlight the interplay between formal leave policies and informal care networks, with important implications for the design of family-friendly policies.

Welcome to the Gray Parade: Pronatal transfers, pensions, and pricing the value of encouraging births in an aging society

With Joseph Kopecky (Trinity College Dublin)

Comparing later retirement and fertility policies in an economy facing demographic decline.

Read paperSlides
Abstract

How should a rapidly aging economy respond to demographic decline? One approach is to adapt pension institutions by extending working lives; another is to mitigate demographic decline through policies that reduce the cost of children. We compare these strategies in an overlapping-generations economy calibrated to South Korea, in which fertility is a household choice, children cost money throughout dependency, and pronatal subsidies are financed through the same payroll tax as pensions. Under the model’s balanced-budget, no-default closure, keeping the retirement age at sixty eventually requires a payroll tax above one hundred percent, whereas an age of sixty-five produces a feasible path with a peak tax of twenty-one percent. Raising the age from sixty-five to sixty-seven is worth a further 4.9% of lifetime consumption. Financed childcare that raises completed fertility from 1.26 to 1.71 adds 1.3% relative to the age-sixty-five baseline; with retirement fixed at sixty, it leaves welfare 12.9% below that baseline. Fertility policy can therefore complement adaptation through later retirement in the model, but it does not replace it. This ranking survives across planner welfare objectives and alternative structures for the cost of children. The retirement-age experiments are stylized policy comparisons rather than an evaluation of Korea’s complete 2025 pension legislation.

Who Gets to Have It All? The Unequal Reversal of the Education–Fertility Gradient

With Fane Groes and Herdis Steingrimsdottir (CBS)

Read paper
Abstract

We examine how the reversal of the education–fertility gradient varies with socioeconomic background and which fertility margins drive it. Using Danish registers, we link completed fertility and education to paternal income rank for women born 1958–1977. Completed fertility among highly educated women, once well below that of low-educated women, now exceeds it. The reversal is concentrated among women from the upper half of the paternal-income distribution. In the lowest quartile, the gradient flattens but remains negative. Decompositions show that changes in the socioeconomic composition of education groups do not explain the reversal, which instead occurs within education-bybackground groups. The reversal is driven by entry into motherhood. Relative to less-educated women, highly educated women become more likely to have a first child in every background group, especially the advantaged, while their fertility conditional on motherhood declines in less advantaged groups. Combining higher education with motherhood remains unequal across backgrounds.

From parents to Citizens: the Intergenerational Transmission of Trust in the State

With Giuseppe Sorrenti (HEC Lausanne) and Enrico Rubolino (CREST)

Abstract

Trust in public institutions shapes citizens’ relationship with the state, but how is it formed and transmitted across generations? Using rich Italian household survey data, we document strong intergenerational transmission of institutional trust. A one standard-deviation difference in average parental trust is associated with almost 2/3 of a standard deviation in children’s trust, even after accounting for individual and family characteristics. Looking within families reveals that parental disagreement is common, weakens intergenerational alignment, and is associated with children aligning more closely with their mothers than their fathers. To investigate whether institutional experiences can shape trust across generations, we exploit the early-2021 expansion of pagoPA, a platform that digitalized payments to public administrations. Our shift share difference-in-differences design combines the timing of this nationwide reform with historical differences between mothers’ and fathers’ post-office contact, a widely used channel for routine payments to public bodies. Families with greater pre-reform differences in parental contact experience larger subsequent increases in both parental and child trust. These findings suggest that institutional trust is formed within the family and that changes in citizens’ encounters with public administrations may have consequences across generations.
